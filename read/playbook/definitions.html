Part II — Shared Definitions & Operating Language

This Playbook uses a set of terms that cut across development, finance, and operational delivery. These definitions are not academic. They are intended to ensure that different actors interpret key concepts in a consistent, actionable way.

2.1 Core investment and partnership terms

Bankability

The extent to which an opportunity can attract financing on the basis of a credible demand signal, identifiable revenue or payment mechanism, appropriate risk allocation, and sufficient preparation. Bankability is not only about financial returns; it is about whether capital can be deployed with an understood and manageable risk profile.

Deployability

The readiness of an opportunity for capital, capability, and institutional commitment to move into execution. A deployable opportunity has clear demand, defined roles, credible delivery systems, risk mitigation, and a pathway to implementation. Deployability precedes and enables bankability.

Bankable demand

Demand that is not only visible but linked to a reliable payment mechanism. It reflects the presence of a payer (user, government, insurer, purchaser, or intermediary) and a structure through which that demand can translate into predictable revenue or repayment.

Project preparation

The set of technical, financial, legal, environmental, and institutional activities required to structure an opportunity into a financeable and implementable project. This includes feasibility analysis, structuring, documentation, procurement readiness, and risk assessment.

Risk allocation

The distribution of risks across actors based on their ability to manage, mitigate, or absorb them. Effective risk allocation aligns incentives, reduces uncertainty, and improves the likelihood that a partnership can reach financial close and perform over time.

Aggregation

The process of pooling multiple small or fragmented opportunities into a larger, standardized portfolio that can be assessed, financed, and managed collectively. Aggregation improves scale, diversifies risk, and increases investor relevance.

Guarantees

Financial instruments that transfer or cover specific risks – such as payment default, political risk, or performance failure – typically provided by public or development institutions to enable private participation or improve financing terms.

Catalytic capital / first-loss capital

Capital that is deployed to absorb higher risk or lower returns in order to enable additional private or commercial capital to enter. First-loss capital is a form of catalytic capital that takes initial losses in a portfolio, improving the risk profile for senior investors.

Standardization

The use of repeatable structures, contracts, data frameworks, and processes to reduce transaction costs and enable replication. Standardization is a key condition for scaling beyond one-off or bespoke transactions.

Predictable delivery networks

Coordinated systems of service providers, infrastructure, and operational actors that deliver goods or services at consistent quality, volume, and performance, supported by defined standards, verification mechanisms, and reliable payment flows.

2.2 CSO and legitimacy terms

Legitimacy

The degree to which institutions, actors, and delivery models are trusted, accepted, and recognized as credible by the communities and stakeholders they serve. Legitimacy affects adoption, compliance, payment behavior, and long-term sustainability.

Community intelligence

Context-specific knowledge about needs, behaviors, constraints, preferences, risks, and informal systems within a community. It informs design, improves targeting, reduces failure risk, and strengthens adoption.

Accountability architecture

The systems, processes, and roles through which actors are held responsible for performance, conduct, and outcomes. This includes monitoring, reporting, feedback loops, enforcement mechanisms, and governance structures.

Social performance

The extent to which a partnership delivers intended social outcomes – such as access, quality, inclusion, and affordability – alongside operational and financial performance. It reflects whether impact objectives are achieved in practice.

Grievance mechanisms

Formal or informal channels through which users, communities, or stakeholders can raise concerns, report issues, and seek resolution. Effective grievance mechanisms strengthen trust, correct failures early, and improve accountability.

Inclusion safeguards

Design features and operational practices that ensure vulnerable or underserved populations are not excluded. This includes affordability measures, access provisions, participation requirements, and monitoring of equity outcomes.

2.3 Strategy and institutional role terms

Strategic fit

The degree to which a partnership opportunity aligns with an institution’s mandate, priorities, capabilities, risk appetite, and time horizon. Strategic fit determines whether an institution should engage and at what level.

Market-shaping role

An institutional role focused on influencing how a market functions, rather than directly delivering services or financing individual projects. This may include setting standards, reducing barriers, improving information, or supporting key enabling conditions.

Ecosystem-building role

An institutional role focused on strengthening the broader system required for partnerships to function. This includes developing intermediaries, building capacity, improving coordination, supporting enabling infrastructure, and fostering collaboration across actors.

Institutional role selection

The process of determining how an institution should participate in a partnership pathway – whether as funder, convener, de-risker, operator, aggregator, purchaser, advocate, or ecosystem builder – based on constraints, capabilities, and strategic intent.

Sustainable market and capital deployment logic

The underlying rationale that explains how an opportunity can support ongoing investment, operational continuity, and market participation over time. It integrates demand, revenue, risk, delivery systems, and institutional commitment.

Portfolio relevance

The extent to which a partnership opportunity contributes meaningfully to an institution’s broader set of activities or investments. It considers scale, replicability, strategic importance, and how the opportunity fits within a wider portfolio rather than as a standalone project.

2.4 Impact Capital Continuum

Grant capital

Non-repayable funding used where activities generate public or social value but do not yet have a credible revenue, repayment, or cost-recovery pathway. Grant capital is often appropriate for inclusion, affordability, community engagement, evidence generation, and early-stage market-building.

Market-building grants

Grant funding used to strengthen the conditions required for markets or partnership pathways to function. This may include demand validation, ecosystem coordination, standards, data systems, provider capacity, policy support, and early pipeline development.

Technical assistance

Non-financial or grant-funded support that strengthens the capacity, readiness, or quality of an opportunity, institution, or market actor. Technical assistance may support feasibility work, financial modelling, legal structuring, procurement preparation, operational improvement, ESG readiness, or provider accreditation.

Catalytic capital

Flexible or risk-tolerant capital used to unlock participation from other funders, investors, or market actors. Catalytic capital may accept higher risk, lower returns, longer timelines, or more flexible terms in order to make a partnership pathway more deployable.

First-loss capital

A form of catalytic capital that absorbs initial losses in a transaction, facility, or portfolio before senior investors are affected. It is often used to improve the risk-return profile of an opportunity and crowd in more risk-averse capital.

Concessional finance

Repayable finance provided on more favorable terms than commercial capital, such as lower interest rates, longer tenors, grace periods, or flexible repayment conditions. It is useful where an opportunity has a credible repayment pathway but still faces affordability, risk, or market-readiness constraints.

Guarantees

Risk-sharing instruments that protect lenders, investors, suppliers, or other actors against specific risks, such as payment default, credit risk, political risk, currency risk, or performance risk. Guarantees should address clearly identified risks and should not be used to compensate for weak project design or absent revenue logic.

Liquidity facilities

Short-term financing or reserve mechanisms used to manage timing gaps between delivery and payment. Liquidity facilities are especially relevant where a payer or purchaser is credible, but payment delays could undermine service continuity or provider participation.

Blended finance

The strategic use of public, philanthropic, concessional, or catalytic resources to mobilize additional private or commercial capital toward development outcomes. Blended finance is most effective when it addresses specific risks or market failures and creates a pathway toward more sustainable financing over time.

Domestic capital

Capital mobilized from local or regional sources, including banks, pension funds, insurers, public funds, corporates, development banks, or local investors. Domestic capital is important for local ownership, currency alignment, market deepening, and reduced dependence on external financing.

Commercial capital

Capital provided on market-based terms by banks, investors, funds, or other financial institutions. Commercial capital typically requires credible cash flows, repayment capacity, risk allocation, legal enforceability, and sufficient transaction size or portfolio scale.

Capital recycling

The process through which capital is repaid, refinanced, or redeployed into new opportunities rather than used once. Capital recycling becomes possible when partnership models generate predictable repayments, performance data, refinancing pathways, or take-out options for early catalytic or concessional capital.

Capital sequencing

The process of matching the right form of capital to the right stage of readiness. The appropriate instrument depends on the maturity of demand, credibility of the payer, allocation of risk, strength of delivery systems, and readiness of institutions to execute. The model chapters apply this logic through model-specific capital pathways, while the Toolkit provides the full capital sequencing tools.

2.5 Stakeholder Archetypes and Operating Roles

Because this Playbook speaks to a broad set of actors, it uses the stakeholder archetypes below consistently. These categories are not rigid: one institution may play multiple roles depending on the partnership model, sector, or stage of implementation.

In this Playbook, development actors, development partners, and institutional actors are used as umbrella terms unless a more specific role is required.

These archetypes help clarify who is needed, what each actor can contribute, and what each actor should not be expected to carry alone. They are used throughout the Playbook to support role selection, partnership design, and deployability assessment.
