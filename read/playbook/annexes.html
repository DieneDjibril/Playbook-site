Annex: Impact Capital Continuum

Purpose of the Continuum

The Impact Capital Continuum describes how different forms of capital may be deployed as an opportunity moves from early-stage development activity toward more deployable, scalable, and financeable partnership pathways.

The Continuum is not a linear funding ladder and should not be interpreted as a requirement that all opportunities progress toward commercial finance. Some activities will remain appropriately grant-funded or publicly financed. The purpose of the Continuum is to help users determine:

  • which form of capital is appropriate at a given stage;
  • what risks remain unresolved;
  • what conditions must be met before additional capital can participate; and
  • when progression to other forms of capital is realistic and justified.

The model-specific capital pathways in Parts VIII–X apply this logic to Aggregation and Warehouse Platforms, Integrated Project Preparation and Guarantee Platforms, and Predictable Delivery Networks.

Grant Capital

When it is appropriate

Grant capital is appropriate where activities generate public or social value but do not yet have a credible revenue, reimbursement, purchasing, or repayment pathway.

Typical uses include:

  • community engagement;
  • inclusion activities;
  • market-building;
  • demand validation;
  • early-stage pilot activities;
  • accountability systems;
  • public-good functions.

What it should not be used for

Grant capital should not be used indefinitely to sustain activities that require a sustainable operating, financing, or ownership model.

Conditions before moving forward

Before additional capital can participate:

  • demand should be better understood;
  • delivery assumptions should be tested;
  • implementation capability should be visible;
  • risks should be identified.

Market-Building Grants

When it is appropriate

Market-building grants strengthen the conditions required for partnership pathways to function.

Typical uses include:

  • standards development;
  • ecosystem coordination;
  • provider readiness;
  • pipeline development;
  • market intelligence;
  • data systems.

What it should not be used for

Market-building grants should not substitute for sustainable financing, purchasing, or ownership arrangements.

Conditions before moving forward

  • demand is visible;
  • coordination mechanisms exist;
  • actors can participate under clear rules and standards.

Technical Assistance

When it is appropriate

Technical assistance supports readiness, capability, and structuring.

Typical uses include:

  • feasibility studies;
  • legal structuring;
  • financial modelling;
  • procurement preparation;
  • operational improvement;
  • accreditation and quality systems.

What it should not be used for

Technical assistance should not compensate for absent demand, weak institutional commitment, or unresolved political barriers.

Conditions before moving forward

  • opportunities are sufficiently prepared;
  • delivery capability is understood;
  • implementation pathways are clear.

Catalytic Capital

When it is appropriate

Catalytic capital helps unlock participation from other actors by absorbing risks that commercial capital cannot accept at an early stage.

Typical uses include:

  • early-stage risk absorption;
  • demonstration effects;
  • crowding in additional investment;
  • supporting deployment where uncertainty remains high.

What it should not be used for

Catalytic capital should not mask weak economics, unclear revenue logic, or poorly prepared opportunities.

Conditions before moving forward

  • risks are identified;
  • risk-sharing logic is clear;
  • a pathway exists for additional participation.

First-Loss Capital

When it is appropriate

First-loss capital protects other investors by absorbing initial losses within a portfolio or financing structure.

Typical uses include:

  • aggregation platforms;
  • portfolio finance;
  • SME finance facilities;
  • blended finance structures.

What it should not be used for

First-loss capital should not be used where the underlying assets, contracts, or cash flows remain fundamentally weak.

Conditions before moving forward

  • portfolio quality is visible;
  • performance data is available;
  • repayment logic is credible.

Guarantees

When it is appropriate

Guarantees should address clearly defined risks, including:

  • payment default;
  • political risk;
  • completion risk;
  • counterparty risk;
  • liquidity risk;
  • regulatory risk.

What it should not be used for

Guarantees should not compensate for poor preparation, weak project design, or absent demand.

Conditions before moving forward

  • risks are explicitly identified;
  • responsibilities are clearly allocated;
  • guarantee triggers and limits are defined.

Concessional Finance

When it is appropriate

Concessional finance is suitable where a repayment pathway exists but risks, affordability constraints, or market immaturity prevent commercial financing.

Typical uses include:

  • transition financing;
  • infrastructure development;
  • working-capital support;
  • provider finance.

What it should not be used for

Concessional finance should not permanently replace sustainable financing arrangements.

Conditions before moving forward

  • repayment performance is demonstrated;
  • risks are reduced;
  • operating systems are functioning effectively.

Blended Finance

When it is appropriate

Blended finance combines concessional, catalytic, philanthropic, public, and commercial resources to support development outcomes.

Typical uses include:

  • crowding in commercial capital;
  • addressing specific market failures;
  • supporting scaling pathways.

What it should not be used for

Blended finance should not be used where the underlying opportunity cannot demonstrate a credible pathway toward sustainability.

Conditions before moving forward

  • risk allocation is clear;
  • capital roles are defined;
  • performance can be measured.

Domestic Capital

When it is appropriate

Domestic capital becomes increasingly relevant when opportunities demonstrate:

  • reliable cash flows;
  • clear governance;
  • repayment discipline;
  • operational credibility.

Sources may include:

  • banks;
  • pension funds;
  • insurers;
  • corporates;
  • public financial institutions;
  • local investors.

What it should not be used for

Domestic capital should not be expected to absorb poorly understood risks without appropriate preparation or safeguards.

Conditions before moving forward

  • performance data exists;
  • payment systems are credible;
  • risk allocation is clear.

Commercial Capital

When it is appropriate

Commercial capital is appropriate where opportunities demonstrate:

  • credible revenue;
  • repayment capacity;
  • enforceable contracts;
  • manageable risk;
  • sufficient scale.

What it should not be used for

Commercial capital should not be expected to finance activities that remain dependent on unresolved structural risks or uncertain payment pathways.

Conditions before moving forward

  • performance history is established;
  • financing structures are proven;
  • revenue and repayment are predictable.

Capital Recycling

When it is appropriate

Capital recycling occurs when capital can be repaid, refinanced, or redeployed into new opportunities.

Typical examples include:

  • repayment of concessional capital;
  • portfolio refinancing;
  • take-out financing;
  • revolving facilities.

What it should not be used for

Capital recycling should not be assumed where repayment pathways are unclear or unsupported by actual performance.

Conditions before moving forward

  • repayment is demonstrated;
  • refinancing mechanisms exist;
  • performance information is reliable;
  • future deployment opportunities are identifiable.

Key Principle

The Impact Capital Continuum is ultimately a deployability framework. Movement between capital types should occur only when the underlying conditions justify it. The objective is not to maximize commercial finance, but to match the right form of capital to the right stage of readiness while strengthening long-term ownership, sustainability, and implementation capability.

Annex: Stakeholder-Specific Recommendations

Purpose

Different actors contribute to deployable partnership pathways in different ways. Partnership success depends not only on selecting the right model, but also on ensuring that each actor plays a role aligned with its mandate, capability, legitimacy, incentives, and risk-bearing capacity.

The recommendations below provide practical guidance on what each stakeholder group should focus on, what it should avoid being expected to do, and which models or institutional roles are most relevant.

CSOs and Community-Facing Organizations

What should this actor do differently?

  • Translate community knowledge into demand intelligence and adoption insights.
  • Participate earlier in partnership design rather than only during implementation.
  • Surface affordability, legitimacy, inclusion, and delivery risks before financing decisions are made.
  • Strengthen accountability, feedback, and grievance systems.
  • Help convert social-impact activity into structured partnership opportunities.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Absorb significant financial, payment, policy, or working-capital risk.
  • Replace government purchasing systems or private-sector operators.
  • Carry responsibility for scale without corresponding authority or resources.

Most relevant models and roles

Models

  • Model 1: Aggregation and Warehouse Platforms
  • Model 3: Predictable Delivery Networks

Roles

  • Demand validator
  • Accountability partner
  • Trust-builder
  • Community engagement lead

Donors and Foundations

What should this actor do differently?

  • Focus grants on deployability constraints rather than perpetual pilots.
  • Support preparation, market-building, system strengthening, and risk reduction.
  • Use catalytic resources to crowd in capability and capital.
  • Define clear pathways from grant funding to sustainable implementation.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Substitute indefinitely for functioning payment systems.
  • Finance recurring delivery without an ownership pathway.
  • Carry all responsibility for coordination.

Most relevant models and roles

Models

  • Model 2: Integrated Project Preparation and Guarantee Platforms
  • Model 1: Aggregation and Warehouse Platforms

Roles

  • Funder
  • Convener
  • Catalyst
  • Ecosystem builder

DFIs and Development Financiers

What should this actor do differently?

  • Focus on preparation, risk allocation, and structured de-risking.
  • Use guarantees and concessional finance selectively.
  • Support domestic capital participation where feasible.
  • Help create pathways toward refinancing and capital recycling.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Finance opportunities that remain poorly prepared.
  • Replace public accountability or core service stewardship.
  • Use risk instruments to compensate for weak design.

Most relevant models and roles

Models

  • Model 2: Integrated Project Preparation and Guarantee Platforms
  • Model 1: Aggregation and Warehouse Platforms

Roles

  • De-risker
  • Capital mobilizer
  • Structured finance partner

Governments and Public Purchasers

What should this actor do differently?

  • Clarify payment commitments and procurement pathways.
  • Strengthen project preparation and approval systems.
  • Improve payment reliability and procurement discipline.
  • Define accountability, quality, and service standards.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Transfer unmanaged risks to providers or investors.
  • Use guarantees without understanding fiscal exposure.
  • Assume private participation automatically improves outcomes.

Most relevant models and roles

Models

  • Model 2: Integrated Project Preparation and Guarantee Platforms
  • Model 3: Predictable Delivery Networks

Roles

  • Public purchaser
  • Regulator
  • Market steward
  • Policy sponsor

SMEs and Private-Sector Operators

What should this actor do differently?

  • Participate in standardization and reporting processes.
  • Demonstrate operational performance and delivery reliability.
  • Build partnerships around verified demand and payment pathways.
  • Engage earlier in preparation processes.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Absorb policy, payment, or political risks beyond its control.
  • Finance public-good functions without compensation.
  • Carry system-level coordination responsibilities alone.

Most relevant models and roles

Models

  • Model 1: Aggregation and Warehouse Platforms
  • Model 3: Predictable Delivery Networks

Roles

  • Operator
  • Service provider
  • Aggregation participant

Investors and Banks

What should this actor do differently?

  • Assess payment systems and institutional readiness, not only project economics.
  • Engage in portfolio approaches where aggregation increases scale.
  • Consider domestic participation pathways and refinancing opportunities.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Finance opportunities with unclear revenue logic.
  • Enter projects where preparation remains incomplete.
  • Replace preparation, coordination, or accountability functions.

Most relevant models and roles

Models

  • Model 1: Aggregation and Warehouse Platforms
  • Model 2: Integrated Project Preparation and Guarantee Platforms

Roles

  • Senior capital provider
  • Domestic capital participant
  • Refinancing partner

Intermediaries and Ecosystem Orchestrators

What should this actor do differently?

  • Move beyond convening to execution support.
  • Reduce transaction friction across actors.
  • Maintain momentum between design, financing, and implementation.
  • Support shared standards, learning, and coordination.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Substitute for decision-making authority.
  • Carry delivery responsibility on behalf of all actors.
  • Act as a long-term owner where ownership should sit elsewhere.

Strategy Leaders and Institutional Decision-Makers

What should this actor do differently?

  • Diagnose the binding constraint before acting.
  • Translate ambition into clear institutional roles.
  • Align resources, incentives, and implementation pathways.
  • Build explicit transition and ownership strategies.

What should this actor not be expected to do?

  • Assume partnership activity automatically creates impact.
  • Rely on tools or frameworks without operational ownership.
  • Remain at the level of strategy without execution accountability.

  • Role selector

Core Principle

The most effective partnerships emerge when actors focus on the functions they are best placed to perform, while avoiding the transfer of responsibilities, risks, and expectations to actors that lack the authority, incentives, capability, legitimacy, or resources to carry them successfully.

Annex: Trade-Offs, Limits, and Appropriate Use

Purpose

The Playbook is designed to help users identify and structure opportunities where partnership pathways can improve delivery, sustainability, accountability, scalability, or financing outcomes. However, not all development activities should be commercialized, and not all partnership opportunities require private-sector participation.

This annex provides a practical framework for assessing when alternative approaches may be more appropriate and when partnership structures may introduce unnecessary costs, risks, or distortions.

The accompanying Toolkit includes a Trade-Off Assessment Checklist that can be used to apply these considerations systematically.

When Grant Funding Remains Appropriate

Grant funding remains appropriate where activities generate significant public or social value but lack a credible revenue, reimbursement, purchasing, or repayment pathway.

Examples may include:

  • community engagement and trust-building;
  • accountability and grievance systems;
  • inclusion initiatives;
  • advocacy and policy reform;
  • market-building activities;
  • early-stage innovation;
  • public-good functions;
  • services for populations that cannot reasonably pay.

Grant funding should not be viewed as a temporary placeholder in all cases. Some functions are appropriately grant-funded because their primary value is social rather than commercial.

Key question

Would introducing repayment expectations, revenue requirements, or commercial returns undermine the core purpose of the activity?

When Public Delivery May Be Preferable

Some services are fundamentally public responsibilities and may be best delivered directly by public institutions.

Public delivery may be preferable where:

  • universal access is a primary objective;
  • strong public accountability is required;
  • market incentives are weak or absent;
  • equity concerns are significant;
  • private participation would add complexity without improving outcomes.

Private actors may still contribute through technology, logistics, maintenance, procurement, infrastructure delivery, or specialist services, without becoming the primary delivery mechanism.

Key question

Does private participation improve delivery performance or simply introduce additional actors into the system?

When Private-Sector Participation May Undermine Equity

Private-sector participation can expand access and improve performance, but it can also create incentives that unintentionally exclude vulnerable groups.

Risks may include:

  • prioritization of more profitable users;
  • exclusion of remote or underserved communities;
  • affordability barriers;
  • service quality differences across income groups;
  • reduced access for populations with limited purchasing power.

These risks do not automatically justify excluding private participation, but they require explicit safeguards.

Key question

Could commercial incentives systematically disadvantage the populations the partnership is intended to serve?

When Transaction Costs Exceed Benefits

Partnership structures create coordination, governance, legal, monitoring, and reporting requirements. In some cases, the costs of structuring a partnership may outweigh the benefits.

Warning signs include:

  • very small opportunity size;
  • limited scale potential;
  • highly localized or one-off interventions;
  • excessive negotiation requirements;
  • disproportionate administrative burden.

In these situations, simpler approaches may be more effective.

Key question

Does the partnership structure create more value than complexity?

When Accountability Risks Cannot Be Managed

Partnerships rely on accountability systems that allow users, funders, providers, and public institutions to monitor performance and address failures.

Partnership approaches may be inappropriate where:

  • performance cannot be verified;
  • users cannot raise concerns safely;
  • grievance systems are absent;
  • oversight capacity is weak;
  • contractual enforcement is unrealistic.

Weak accountability can undermine both impact and legitimacy.

Key question

Can stakeholders realistically detect, report, and correct failures?

When Commercialization Creates Mission Drift

Commercial participation can support sustainability, but it can also shift attention away from intended development outcomes.

Mission drift may occur when:

  • financial performance becomes the primary goal;
  • social objectives become secondary;
  • incentives prioritize growth over inclusion;
  • activities are redesigned primarily to attract investors;
  • accountability shifts away from intended beneficiaries.

Mission drift is particularly important for CSOs, foundations, social enterprises, and mission-driven partnerships.

Key question

Would commercialization change the fundamental purpose of the intervention?

Decision Principle

The purpose of private-sector collaboration is not to maximize commercialization. It is to improve outcomes, strengthen systems, expand access, increase sustainability, and create more deployable pathways where appropriate.

The central question is therefore:

Does private-sector participation create additional public value, or would another approach achieve the same objective more effectively, equitably, and sustainably?

Where the answer is unclear, users should apply the Trade-Off Assessment Checklist included in the Companion Toolkit before selecting a model or structuring a partnership.
