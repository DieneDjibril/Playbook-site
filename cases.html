Nigeria · Food & Agriculture

Babban Gona Smallholder Aggregation Platform

Model 1 · Aggregation & Warehouse Platforms
200,000+Farmers across 13 states
≈USD 85MCapital mobilized
≈99%Repayment rate over a decade+
≈USD 10MCitibank facility (2022, first local-currency commercial lending)

The challenge

Fragmentation kept smallholder agriculture outside the reach of formal finance, weakened market efficiency, and limited farmers' commercial opportunities.

The response

Founded in 2012, an integrated aggregation platform organizes farmers into standardized Trust Groups — bundling input financing, agronomic training, input distribution, harvest aggregation, warehousing, and market access in one coordinated system, with Nestlé as a long-term buyer.

Why this case illustrates Model 1

  • Market visibility — dispersed producers become one coherent, transparent market.
  • Quality standards — common practices make the system commercially reliable.
  • Investability — reduced transaction costs create a financeable unit, attracting catalytic, sovereign, and commercial capital.
  • Scaling — producers are added within a common framework without proportional coordination costs.

Kenya · Energy

KCB Clean Cooking & Clean Energy Transition

Model 2 · Project Preparation & Guarantee Platform
47 → 266Schools in pilot progression
3,213Boarding schools targeted, 47 counties (approved scale-up)
USD 96.9MGCF facility · USD 150M AfDB facility
≈9.75%Program rate vs ≈17.65% commercial benchmark

The challenge

Schools depended on firewood, creating financial pressure, environmental harm, and serious public-health risks — a visible opportunity that could not become investable at scale.

The response

Kenya Commercial Bank launched an initiative to move learning institutions from biomass to cleaner cooking systems. Following a pilot that grew from 47 to 266 schools, national scale-up was approved — the program is still ongoing, with the 3,213-school target approved but not yet fully achieved.

Why this case illustrates Model 2

  • Project preparation — validated feasibility and clarified responsibilities, answering unbankable demand and the missing pipeline.
  • Risk mitigation — credible mechanisms addressed unallocated implementation risk.
  • Coordination — a single accountable platform aligned incentives and maintained momentum.
  • Capital mobilization — the outcome: public, private, concessional, and climate finance could engage.

Ethiopia · Health & Nutrition

Local Production of Ready-to-Use Therapeutic Foods

Model 3 · Predictable Delivery Networks
USD 300,000Catalytic equipment investment
40 → 80MT/month to MT/day production capacity
10,000+ MTSupplied annually
UNICEFAnchor purchaser · WFP & USAID buyers today

The challenge

Humanitarian financing paid for the same imported therapeutic foods year after year. Long supply chains raised costs, extended timelines, and left the nutrition response exposed to external disruption.

The response

Starting from a 2007 partnership, UNICEF partnered with Hilina Enriched Foods, Nutriset, and donor partners to establish Ethiopia's first large-scale local manufacturing platform for therapeutic foods — converting recurring humanitarian spend into sustainable domestic production.

Why this case illustrates Model 3

  • Demand structuring — UNICEF as anchor purchaser turned volatile procurement into a signal a manufacturer could invest against.
  • Capability building — technology transfer from Nutriset delivered production, quality, and manufacturing standards.
  • Catalytic financing — donor-financed equipment, repaid through product deliveries, created capacity where commercial finance was unavailable.

Kenya · Energy

Lake Turkana Wind Power Project

Model 2 · Project Preparation & Guarantee Platform
€623MAt financial close (2014)
310 MWWind farm capacity
1,481 GWhGenerated in 2023 (≈11% of Kenya's electricity that year)
2014Financial close

The challenge

Hydro dependence created supply insecurity, fiscal stress, and a widening gap between energy ambition and delivery. A technically viable opportunity lacked the architecture to become an investable asset.

The response

The LTWP consortium partnered with the Government of Kenya, Kenya Power, KETRACO, and a consortium of DFIs to prepare and de-risk one of Africa's most ambitious renewable energy projects.

Why this case illustrates Model 2

  • Project preparation — established technical, financial, legal, environmental, and contractual foundations.
  • Risk mitigation — allocated political, commercial, technical, and operational risks to the actors best placed to manage them.
  • Coordination — aligned interdependent public and private institutions into a single delivery mechanism.
  • Capital mobilization — DFIs, commercial lenders, and export credit agencies financed what no single institution could support alone.
