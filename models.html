Three partnership models

Different constraints require fundamentally different responses. Applying the wrong model — or the right model to the wrong problem — leads to stalled partnerships, inefficient capital, or repeated pilot cycles without scale.

1
Constraint: fragmentation

Aggregation & Warehouse Platforms

Pools multiple small or fragmented opportunities into a larger, standardized portfolio that can be assessed, financed, and managed collectively — the direct answer to opportunities without visibility.

Four complementary functions

  • Market visibility — organizes dispersed producers into a single, transparent commercial structure.
  • Quality standards — common practices and performance monitoring improve commercial reliability.
  • Investability — reduced transaction costs create a financeable unit.
  • Scaling — growth within one operational framework, not isolated transactions.

Best when

Many small producersNo aggregation ownerWeak standardizationHigh transaction costs
Live case

Babban Gona organizes farmers into standardized Trust Groups, bundling input financing, training, aggregation, warehousing, and market access into one system — now one of Nigeria's largest single maize producers.

2
Constraint: underdeveloped opportunity

Integrated Project Preparation & Guarantee Platform

Rather than focusing on a single barrier, the model strengthens the broader conditions required for large-scale implementation. Capital mobilization is the outcome, not the starting point.

Four complementary functions

  • Project preparation — validates assumptions and clarifies responsibilities before execution, turning strategic opportunities into propositions partners can engage with confidence.
  • Risk mitigation — credible mechanisms show how risks will be understood, managed, and shared.
  • Coordination — a single accountable pathway aligns incentives and maintains momentum.
  • Capital mobilization — public, private, concessional, and climate finance engage once conditions are set.

Best when

Unbankable demandMissing pipelineUnallocated riskNo coordination owner
Live cases

KCB's Clean Cooking transition for schools (47 → 266 schools, national scale-up approved) and Lake Turkana Wind Power (€623m, Kenya's largest private investment at financial close) both apply this model.

3
Constraint: fragmented delivery

Predictable Delivery Networks

Rather than creating new providers or technologies, the model organizes existing capabilities into a coordinated delivery system with reliable standards, verification, and payment flows.

Four complementary functions

  • Demand structuring — an anchor purchaser converts volatile demand into a commercial signal a provider can invest against.
  • Capability building — strengthens existing providers through technology transfer and quality systems.
  • Catalytic financing — removes the initial investment barrier while preserving commercial discipline.
  • Coordination & verification — sustains delivery continuity, quality, and payment reliability.

Best when

Unpredictable demandCapability gapImport dependenceFragmented providers
Live case

UNICEF as anchor purchaser plus technology transfer from Nutriset enabled Hilina to supply 10,000+ MT of therapeutic foods annually — reducing import dependence in Ethiopia.

Before you scale

System readiness beats model design

A model can be conceptually strong but still stall if the systems beneath it are too weak to support delivery, payment, coordination, and learning.

Financial & regulatory

Payment rails, procurement, regulatory approvals, and governance systems.

Operational & data

Logistics, delivery capacity, data infrastructure, and verification mechanisms.

Relational & accountability

Trust, community engagement, feedback loops, grievance channels, and protection.

Key implication: scaling is not only a function of model design — it is a function of system readiness.
