The Framework

Partnerships underperform not because opportunity, impact, or capital is absent — but because development activity is rarely translated into forms that partners can assess, structure, finance, scale, and repeat.

The core idea

What is deployability?

Deployability refers to the conditions that allow capital, institutional commitment, and private-sector participation to move into an opportunity with confidence. A deployable opportunity has more than impact potential.

Deployability precedes and enables bankability. The binding constraint is often not capital scarcity — it is whether the opportunity has been organized in a way that lets different actors participate sustainably.

Why partnerships stall

The translation gap

Different actors describe the same opportunity in different languages. These perspectives are not incompatible — strong partnerships require all of them — but without a common operating logic, opportunities stay stuck between worlds.

Development actors

Beneficiaries, needs, inclusion, impact, theory of change, community trust, program sustainability.

Financiers & private sector

Clients, revenue, margins, repayment, risk, return, cash flow, offtake, guarantees, scale.

Governments

Policy alignment, fiscal exposure, procurement, public value, accountability, political feasibility.

A program may have beneficiaries but no defined payer. It may show need but not bankable demand.
The language gap is not a communications problem alone — it is a structuring problem.

Ask early, not after

Five translation questions

Asked at the start, these questions convert social-impact activity into partnership architecture — making complexity visible and manageable.

1
Benefit

Who benefits?

Not only who needs the intervention, but who uses it, values it, depends on it, or gains from its continued delivery.

2
Payment

Who pays?

The person who benefits may not be the one who pays. Without a credible payment logic, demand stays visible but not bankable.

3
Risk

What risk blocks capital?

Demand, payment, political, currency, procurement, construction, or performance risk — each needs a different instrument.

4
Sustainability

What makes it sustainable?

Reliable delivery capacity, clear standards, sufficient margins, credible governance, and a pathway to institutional ownership.

5
Repeatability

How does it repeat?

Scale depends on standardization, templates, data, benchmarks, and models that adapt across locations without redesign.

Sequencing capital to readiness

The Impact Capital Continuum

The purpose is not to push every opportunity toward commercial finance, but to ensure capital follows readiness rather than substituting for structure.

01

Grant & market-building

Builds trust, validates demand, supports inclusion, strengthens early market conditions.

02

Technical assistance

Strengthens capacity, feasibility, structuring, and procurement readiness.

03

Catalytic & first-loss

Absorbs higher risk or lower returns to crowd in more risk-averse capital.

04

Guarantees & concessional

Covers specific risks and improves financing terms where repayment is credible.

05

Domestic & commercial

Market-based capital as demand, payment, and delivery systems become credible.

Capital sequencing matches the right form of capital to the right stage of readiness — depending on the maturity of demand, credibility of the payer, allocation of risk, and readiness of institutions to execute.
