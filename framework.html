The Framework

The Framework

Partnerships underperform not because opportunity, impact, or capital is absent — but because development activity is rarely translated into forms that partners can assess, structure, finance, scale, and repeat.

The core idea

What is deployability?

Deployability refers to the conditions that allow capital, institutional commitment, and private-sector participation to move into an opportunity with confidence. A deployable opportunity has more than impact potential.

Deployability precedes and enables bankability. The binding constraint is often not capital scarcity — it is whether the opportunity has been organized in a way that lets different actors participate sustainably.
Why partnerships stall

The translation gap

Different actors describe the same opportunity in different languages. These perspectives are not incompatible — strong partnerships require all of them — but without a common operating logic, opportunities stay stuck between worlds.

One shared problem

A program may have beneficiaries but no defined payer. It may show need but not bankable demand. The language gap is not a communications problem alone — it is a structuring problem.

Demand & Visibility

Whether the need or demand can be seen, evidenced and sized by those who might buy, fund or invest.

What this may indicate

The need may be real and well understood by those close to it, but not yet evidenced or sized in terms that buyers, funders or investors can assess. Visible need does not automatically become bankable demand.

Questions to investigate

  • Who benefits — who uses, values, depends on, or gains from the intervention’s continued delivery?
  • Is the demand visible, validated, and understood from the perspective of users or communities?
  • Are beneficiaries, users, clients, and payers the same or different actors?

What would challenge this hypothesis?

If demand is already evidenced and sized, but no one has committed to buy or pay, the constraint may sit in Payment Credibility & Risk instead. Weak investor interest is a symptom — on its own, it does not show that demand is the binding constraint.

Payment Credibility & Risk

Whether visible demand is backed by a credible commitment to buy or pay — and whether risks sit with those able to carry them.

What this may indicate

Demand may be visible, but not yet connected to a credible payment, purchasing, reimbursement, offtake, or revenue mechanism — or risks may be carried by actors who cannot manage, price, or absorb them.

Questions to investigate

  • Is the payer clearly identified, with the budget, liquidity, and willingness to pay?
  • Are commitments written, budgeted, or contract-backed — or only verbal?
  • Which risks are present, who is carrying each one, and are they best placed to manage it?
  • Can payment be verified through claims, invoices, outputs, or performance data?

What would challenge this hypothesis?

If a clearly identified payer has budget- or contract-backed commitments and pays reliably, the blockage may lie elsewhere — for example in preparation. And if preparation is incomplete, guarantees alone will not make the opportunity financeable.

Preparation & Readiness

Whether the opportunity is developed enough — studies, costings, standards, structure — for partners to assess and act on it.

What this may indicate

The opportunity may be identified and even prioritized, but not yet translated into an executable structure. A list of opportunities is not the same as an investable pipeline.

Questions to investigate

  • How far have feasibility, financial, operational, and legal structuring progressed?
  • Are procurement, approval, and implementation steps clear?
  • Are delivery assumptions and operating costs documented?
  • Does the opportunity rest on repeatable structures, or on one-off relationships?

What would challenge this hypothesis?

If the opportunity is well prepared but no credible payer stands behind it, further studies will not unlock it. Repeated studies without progress may also signal an incentive issue rather than a capacity gap — ask who benefits from delay.

Coordination & Fragmentation

Whether someone owns the process of moving things forward, and whether many small or separate efforts can add up.

What this may indicate

Several actors may be committed, yet no one is responsible for moving the opportunity from concept to structure, financing, implementation, and learning — so the work fragments across institutions.

Questions to investigate

  • Is there a clear coordination owner responsible for moving the opportunity to execution?
  • Which institutions approve the project — and who can block progress even without formally leading it?
  • Are handoffs between program design, financing, procurement, and delivery clear?
  • Are any actors advantaged by unclear roles or opaque processes?

What would challenge this hypothesis?

If a clear owner is already maintaining momentum across decision points, slow progress may point to incomplete preparation or weak payment commitments instead. Where payer credibility is weak, additional convening will not unlock scale.

Legitimacy & Trust

Whether the people and communities involved trust, accept and help shape the work — and can hold it to account.

What this may indicate

Community trust, social acceptance, and user adoption may have been assumed rather than built into the partnership. Legitimacy is built over time through relationships, responsiveness, accountability, and credible delivery.

Questions to investigate

  • Are trust, legitimacy, and adoption conditions strong enough to support implementation?
  • Which actors are already trusted by users — and are they involved early enough to shape design?
  • Are inclusion, affordability, and community-level risks identified early?
  • Are feedback, grievance, and accountability mechanisms built into the operating model?

What would challenge this hypothesis?

If communities already trust and use the service and feedback mechanisms work, hesitation from partners may reflect payment or preparation gaps instead. But weak legitimacy can make implementation fail even where financing is available.

Sequencing capital to readiness

The Impact Capital Continuum

The purpose is not to push every opportunity toward commercial finance, but to ensure capital follows readiness rather than substituting for structure.

1 2 3 4 5

Grant & market-building

Builds trust, validates demand, supports inclusion, strengthens early market conditions.

Technical assistance

Catalytic & first-loss

Absorbs higher risk or lower returns to crowd in more risk-averse capital.

Guarantees & concessional

Covers specific risks and improves financing terms where repayment is credible.

Domestic & commercial

Market-based capital as demand, payment, and delivery systems become credible.

Capital sequencing matches the right form of capital to the right stage of readiness — depending on the maturity of demand, credibility of the payer, allocation of risk, and readiness of institutions to execute.
